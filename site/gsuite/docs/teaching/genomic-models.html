4. Pedigree, genomic and combined relationship models

1 What distinguishes one genomic model from another?

Genomic prediction models differ in their assumptions about genetic effects, their reference populations and the information they use. The choice of marker-effect or animal-effect equations is sometimes only a change of representation. Changing the prior distribution, relationship kernel or genomic blending generally changes the statistical model.

Advanced notes: contents · Abbreviations

This chapter builds on the general framework. Write \(y=X\beta+Za+e\), with additive genetic values \(a\), residual covariance \(R\), and genetic covariance \(K_a\). The prediction target is the additive genetic value relative to a declared base. Total genotypic value, marker association and individual future phenotype are different targets.

Family Main assumption or information source Distinction
Pedigree BLUP \(K_a=\sigma_a^2 A\) Pedigree expectation of additive relationships
SNP-BLUP / ridge regression Gaussian marker effects Marker-effect representation of an additive kernel
GBLUP \(K_a=\sigma_a^2 G\) Animal-effect representation of that kernel
Weighted or multi-kernel GBLUP Marker weights or several genetic components Changes shrinkage across genomic directions
ssGBLUP / ssSNPBLUP Joint pedigree and genomic information Includes non-genotyped animals in one model
Bayesian variable-selection or mixture models Non-Gaussian marker priors Allows heterogeneous, possibly sparse marker effects
Dominance and interaction models Additional non-additive kernels Changes the genetic target and decomposition
Multitrait and genotype-by-environment interaction (G×E) models Covariance across traits or environments Extends how genetic information is shared

These families differ in statistical assumptions, prediction targets and inference. Some are exact reparameterizations; others change the model.

2 Pedigree BLUP as the relationship baseline

The sparse pedigree-inverse construction is due to Henderson (1976).

For an additive animal model, \(K_a=\sigma_a^2A\). If animal \(i\) has known sire \(s\) and dam \(d\), pedigree recursion gives \(A_{ij}=(A_{sj}+A_{dj})/2\) for earlier animals and \(A_{ii}=1+A_{sd}/2\). Unknown parent contributions follow the specified founder assumptions. Animals must be topologically ordered for this recursion, while external identities must remain stable across all outputs.

A structural representation writes \(a=P_{\rm ped}a+\varepsilon\), where \(P_{\rm ped}\) contains known parental half-contributions and \(\varepsilon\) contains independent Mendelian innovations under the pedigree model, taking \(\sigma_a^2=1\) for this relationship construction. With their correctly computed variance diagonal \(D_{\rm ped}\) on the relationship scale,

\[ A^{-1}=(I-P_{\rm ped})^\top D_{\rm ped}^{-1}(I-P_{\rm ped}). \]

Parental inbreeding changes innovation variances. The simple diagonal values for unrelated, non-inbred founders must not be applied indiscriminately to inbred pedigrees. This representation explains why pedigree precision can be sparse even though the relationship matrix itself is dense.

For founder mean and covariance assumptions, genomic compatibility and crossbred prediction targets, see genetic bases and crossbreeding.

Pedigree best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) uses expected relationships under this inheritance model. Genomic models can capture variation in realized marker sharing around those pedigree expectations; their base, scale and target must still be specified.

3 Marker coding and the genetic reference population

Let \(M_{ij}\in\{0,1,2\}\) count a consistently chosen allele at marker \(j\). For reference allele frequencies \(p_j\), define

\[ W_{ij}=M_{ij}-2p_j,\qquad d=\sum_j2p_j(1-p_j),\qquad G=WW^\top/d. \]

This common construction uses one overall normalization. It differs from standardizing every marker to unit variance before taking a cross-product. Both are legitimate covariance specifications, but they imply different marker weighting. Rare variants can receive much greater relative weight under per-marker standardization.

Frequencies calculated in the training sample, the full genotyped population or an external base need not give the same \(W\). Under mean imputation at these same frequencies, a missing centered dosage is zero. That is an imputation assumption, not evidence that the genotype is observed. Marker filtering, imputation and frequency estimation should be recorded separately.

Reversing an allele consistently changes one column of \(W\) to its negative. Its contribution to \(WW^\top\) is unchanged, while the corresponding estimated marker effect changes sign. Inconsistent allele alignment between training and prediction data changes predictions.

4 SNP-BLUP and GBLUP: an exact covariance equivalence

In single-nucleotide polymorphism best linear unbiased prediction (SNP-BLUP),

\[ y=X\beta+ZWm+e,\qquad m\sim N(0,\sigma_m^2I). \]

The induced genetic covariance is

\[ K_a=\sigma_m^2WW^\top=\sigma_a^2G, \qquad \sigma_m^2=\sigma_a^2/d. \]

Consequently SNP-BLUP and genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) give identical fitted genetic values when their covariance scales, fixed-effect spaces, observations and residual models match. This is the equivalence examined by (VanRaden, 2008).

With one record per genotyped animal and \(R=\sigma_e^2I\), marker equations contain the penalty \(\lambda I\), where

\[ \lambda=\frac{\sigma_e^2}{\sigma_m^2} =\frac{d\sigma_e^2}{\sigma_a^2}. \]

The factor \(d\) must not disappear when transferring variances between parameterizations. An intercept is normally unpenalized.

For a new individual with centered genotype row \(w_*^\top\),

\[ \hat a_*=w_*^\top\hat m =\operatorname{Cov}(a_*,y)V^{-1}(y-X\hat\beta). \]

This expression also clarifies kernel prediction: predictions for a new individual require training-to-new cross-covariances, not just the training kernel. Compatible uncertainty additionally needs the target’s prior variance.

\(G\) is often singular because of centering, limited marker rank or duplicate genotypes. A Gaussian model with a positive-semidefinite genetic covariance can still be valid when \(R\) makes \(V\) positive definite. The marginal formula or marker model then remains usable without an ordinary inverse of \(G\). Adding a ridge solely to permit inversion is not an exact reparameterization of the original singular model.

4.1 Why use one representation rather than another?

An explicit genomic matrix for \(n_g\) genotyped animals costs \(O(n_g^2)\) storage. A marker formulation can apply \(W\) and \(W^\top\) without constructing it, including from packed dosage data. Conversely, a retained relationship factor may make repeated solves attractive when its dimension and fill are manageable. Trait count, missingness, factor reuse and uncertainty requests also matter. Comparing only equation counts does not determine which representation is faster.

5 Weighted markers and several genomic components

With \(m\sim N(0,\tau^2D)\) for a nonnegative diagonal \(D\),

\[ K_a=\tau^2WDW^\top. \]

Fixed externally supplied weights still define a Gaussian mixed model. Weights estimated adaptively from the same phenotypes create a larger estimation procedure; treating them as known afterwards omits their estimation uncertainty.

For biological feature definitions, annotation overlap and feature-dependent marker priors, see Chapter 14.

A multi-kernel model instead writes

\[ a=\sum_k a_k,\qquad a_k\sim N(0,\sigma_k^2G_k),\qquad K_a=\sum_k\sigma_k^2G_k. \]

Possible partitions include chromosomes, functional annotations or variant classes. Each kernel needs its own scale convention. Highly similar kernels can leave component variances weakly identified even when total genetic prediction is stable. Overlapping annotations do not automatically yield independent biological variance contributions: the model defines how that overlap is allocated.

6 Single-step models: retaining non-genotyped animals

Partition the pedigree matrix into non-genotyped (\(n\)) and genotyped (\(g\)) animals. The pedigree conditional model can be written

\[ a_n=T a_g+\varepsilon_n,\qquad T=A_{ng}A_{gg}^{-1},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_n)=\sigma_a^2C_n, \]

where \(C_n=A_{nn}-A_{ng}A_{gg}^{-1}A_{gn}\). Retain this conditional distribution but replace the marginal genotyped covariance by \(\operatorname{Var}(a_g)=\sigma_a^2G_b\). The resulting joint relationship is

\[ H=\begin{bmatrix} C_n+TG_bT^\top&TG_b\\G_bT^\top&G_b \end{bmatrix}. \]

For nonsingular matrices this gives

\[ H^{-1}=A^{-1}+E_g^\top(G_b^{-1}-A_{gg}^{-1})E_g. \]

Here \(E_g\) selects genotyped animals from the complete animal vector. This provides a common basis for single-step genomic BLUP (ssGBLUP) and its marker-effect representation, single-step SNP-BLUP (ssSNPBLUP). Phenotypes on non-genotyped relatives remain in the model; they are not simply discarded or treated as additional genotypes. See (Christensen and Lund, 2010).

An ssSNPBLUP formulation expresses the genomic part through marker effects and retains the additional pedigree-related terms needed to reproduce \(H\). It is not ordinary SNP-BLUP with missing genotype rows filled by zero. Equivalence requires matching the complete latent covariance, including blending, adjustment and any additional genetic component.

6.1 Marginal and conditional pedigree precision are different

Partition animals into non-genotyped (\(n\)) and genotyped (\(g\)), and write \(Q=A^{-1}\). The marginal precision for the genotyped breeding values is

\[ A_{gg}^{-1}=Q_{gg}-Q_{gn}Q_{nn}^{-1}Q_{ng}. \]

It is not generally the principal block \(Q_{gg}\). The latter is the conditional precision of \(a_g\) given \(a_n\). Applying the Schur complement can involve a solve with \(Q_{nn}\) without constructing the dense marginal inverse. This distinction is essential when interpreting timings labelled “pedigree solve” or comparing apparently similar single-step implementations.

6.2 Adjustment, pedigree blending and identity ridge

One moment adjustment is \(G_*=\alpha\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top+\gamma G\), chosen to align specified pedigree and genomic moments. Blending then might be

\[ G_b=(1-w)G_*+wA_{gg}. \]

The model used for our four-trait comparison has \(w=0.05\), adjustment of \(G\) to \(A\), and no independent identity ridge. In contrast, \((1-w)G_*+wI\) assigns the additional variation to independent genotyped-animal directions. It is not the same as 5% pedigree blending.

Without the adjustment intercept, a pedigree blend admits the representation \(a_g=Wm+r_g\) with independent \(r_g\) having covariance \(w\sigma_a^2A_{gg}\) and an appropriately scaled marker covariance. An adjustment intercept contributes an additional rank-one covariance term. It must be represented consistently; a negative adjustment intercept cannot be interpreted on its own as the variance of an independent scalar effect.

The pedigree block \(A_{gg}^{-1}\) is generally dense even when \(A^{-1}\) is sparse. Schur-complement solves or equivalent latent systems avoid explicitly forming that dense inverse. Their cost is real model algebra, distinct from the cost of decoding markers.

6.3 APY and other approximations

The algorithm for proven and young (APY) partitions genotyped animals into a core and a remaining (noncore) group. The name is historical: the core need not consist exclusively of older or proven animals. It retains a core-dependent conditional prediction and approximates the conditional covariance among remaining animals, typically by a diagonal. Thus an APY relationship approximation can change the target model; its predictions depend on core choice and approximation quality. It should not be described as merely a different preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) preconditioner for an unchanged exact system. An approximate operator used only inside a preconditioner is a separate case. For the original recursive construction see Misztal et al. (2014); for an applied treatment of the core/noncore construction see Garcia et al. (2020).

7 Bayesian marker-effect distributions

Gaussian SNP-BLUP assigns one common marker variance on the chosen coding scale. The original BayesA/B models allow heterogeneous marker effects (Meuwissen et al., 2001). Important families include:

Family Representative prior Implication
BayesA Gaussian effects with marker-specific inverse-chi-squared variance mixing Heavy-tailed marginal effects; no point mass at zero
BayesB A point mass at zero plus a heavy-tailed active component Selection and marker-specific active shrinkage
BayesC-type models A point mass at zero plus a common Gaussian active component Selection with a shared active-effect variance
BayesR-type models A finite mixture of zero and several Gaussian variance classes Several degrees of shrinkage

The precise prior includes mixture probabilities, variance scales and their hyperpriors. Names alone are insufficient to reproduce an analysis; some implementations fix a quantity that others estimate. BayesC-type extensions are described by Habier et al. (2011). For BayesR, see Erbe et al. (2012) and Moser et al. (2015).

Conditional on mixture assignments and variance parameters, parts of these models are Gaussian. Integrating over those unknowns gives a non-Gaussian posterior and generally nonlinear prediction in \(y\). Matching the prior’s second moment to a GBLUP covariance therefore does not make the posterior predictions equivalent. Posterior mean, posterior mode, variable-selection probability and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) draw are also different outputs.

Posterior inclusion probabilities describe marker inclusion under a declared prior and linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure. They are not automatically probabilities that a marker is causal. Prediction performance does not establish fine-mapping resolution.

8 Beyond additive, single-trait prediction

An additive/dominance genomic covariance construction and its interpretation are developed by Vitezica et al. (2013).

8.1 Dominance and epistasis

An additive-plus-dominance model uses independent effects with \(K=\sigma_a^2G_a+\sigma_d^2G_d\). Dominance coding must be defined relative to allele frequencies and the intended statistical decomposition. Simply adding squared dosage columns does not specify an orthogonal dominance model.

A Hadamard-product kernel such as \(G_a\circ G_a\) can represent an interaction covariance, but its interpretation depends on coding, centering and whether self-interactions and lower-order effects are included. Kernel labels are not sufficient evidence that estimated components correspond to unique biological mechanisms.

An additive breeding value concerns transmission to offspring under the specified model. Adding dominance or interaction predictions produces a different target, often a component of an individual’s total genotypic value.

8.2 Multitrait, reaction norms and factor structure

For a common additive kernel and trait-major stacking, \(K_a=\Sigma_a\otimes G\) (or \(\Sigma_a\otimes H\) in single-step). Genetic correlations share information across traits but do not guarantee that adding any trait improves prediction in every population.

Reaction norms model genetic coefficients over an environmental basis. If \(a_i(z)=b(z)^\top u_i\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(u_i,u_j)=G_{ij}\Sigma_u\), then

\[ \operatorname{Cov}\{a_i(z),a_j(z')\} =G_{ij}b(z)^\top\Sigma_u b(z'). \]

A factor-analytic trait/environment covariance, \(\Sigma_a=\Lambda\Lambda^\top+\Psi\), reduces the covariance parameterization and imposes structure. It is not automatically the same as a full unstructured multitrait model. Cross-environment prediction depends on the environmental information available at prediction time as well as the genetic kernel.

8.3 Genomic technologies and uncertain input genotypes

Low-coverage sequencing and imputation expand the ways genomic information can be obtained; Lamb et al. (2021) provide a cattle prediction proof of concept. Their availability does not make uncertain dosages exact measurements. Under a simple zero-mean prior with independent marker effects of fixed variance \(\tau^2\), also independent of the genotype data \(\mathcal D_g\), the induced covariance is

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(a\mid\mathcal D_g) =\tau^2 E(WW^\top\mid\mathcal D_g), \]

where centering frequencies and scaling are held fixed. Substituting \(\bar W=E(W\mid\mathcal D_g)\) instead uses \(\tau^2\bar W\bar W^\top\) and omits

\[ \tau^2\sum_j\operatorname{Cov}(W_{\cdot j}\mid\mathcal D_g). \]

This is an illustrative covariance identity, not a complete likelihood for sequence data or a claim that every prediction requires full genotype integration. It shows what must be assessed when dosage uncertainty is consequential. Shared imputation uncertainty can also connect animals.

Structural variants, changing variant sets and breed-specific frequencies likewise require explicit representation and base conventions. Richer functional information then changes the prior on these variants, rather than repairing inconsistent allele alignment or scaling. See functional information and genetic bases.

9 Choosing and assessing a genomic model

For an evaluation, record the scientific target, training and prediction populations, marker coding and frequencies, kernel normalization, covariance parameters, adjustment/blending rule and treatment of non-genotyped animals. Specify whether covariance parameters are supplied, estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) or integrated in Bayesian inference. Computational choices should preserve the intended model or make the approximation and its consequences explicit.

Qualification should connect three levels: an independent small covariance reference, a representative model fit with original-equation residual checks, and prediction validation designed for the intended deployment. Randomly splitting close relatives can answer a different question from prediction across generations or populations. Hyperparameters, marker selection and phenotype-derived weights must be learned inside the training procedure.
