16. Drivers shaping the future of breeding

Advanced notes: contents

1 A connected change in the breeding system

Breeding is likely to be shaped by the interaction of functional genomics, automated phenotyping, machine learning, lower-cost genomic information and changes in farming and breeding organisations. These drivers change more than the size of a prediction problem. They influence which traits are observed, which populations contribute information, who controls access, and how rapidly genetic decisions can be evaluated and revised.

This chapter presents a perspective as of November 2026. Published examples establish what has been demonstrated; the implications and scenarios below are reasoned expectations, not forecasts of adoption rates or guaranteed genetic gain. The current-developments chapter examines specific methods. Here the focus is how developments combine to reshape the breeding system, including its scientific and software requirements.

Climate, disease pressure, welfare, environmental constraints, labour and market demands also determine the direction of change. Better measurement and prediction do not decide which outcomes should be rewarded. A breeding objective must translate these priorities into explicit targets and trade-offs.

Driver What changes Consequence for breeding and inference
Functional and regulatory atlases Biological information connecting variants, tissues and molecular traits More informative priors and hypotheses, with transfer and causal interpretation to establish
Sensors, robots, imaging and other phenotyping technologies Recording intensity, trait definitions and environmental coverage Longitudinal, multitrait and measurement models become increasingly important
ML and AI Feature extraction, representations, prediction and software development Hybrid pipelines need calibrated uncertainty, independent validation and version control
Lower-cost genomic technologies Who can be genotyped and which variants can be interrogated Reference design, imputation, population coverage and uncertainty become central
Structural change in farms and breeding organisations Data ownership, connectedness, scale and delivery of genetics Governance and deployment conditions become part of the statistical design

2 Functional genomics: from association to informed models

FarmGTEx is an international programme to map genetic regulation of molecular traits across farm-animal tissues and populations (Fang et al., 2025). It extends the information available beyond marker–performance associations towards tissue, expression and splicing contexts. The cattle regulatory atlas discussed in genomic-feature priors provides an earlier concrete example (Liu et al., 2022). Related work is developing in plants: Lei et al. (2025) analyse a multitissue maize transcriptome resource and its regulatory associations. These are related scientific directions, not identical experimental designs.

The opportunity is to connect a variant to a molecular process and then to a breeding trait. This can inform marker-effect priors, groups of covariance components, candidate mechanisms and experimental priorities. It may be particularly valuable when information has to transfer between populations or when a trait is expensive to measure. Within a well-connected reference population, ordinary markers can already tag much of the relevant variation; additional biological interpretation need not produce a large accuracy gain.

A regulatory association is not proof that expression mediates the production trait. Linkage disequilibrium, shared regulation, tissue sampling and population structure can produce alternative explanations. Colocalisation, fine-mapping, perturbation experiments and validation in relevant tissues and environments provide different forms of evidence. They should not be replaced by a single annotation score described as a causal probability.

For model development, a useful direction is partial pooling of biological information: let annotations influence prior distributions while retaining uncertainty and a background genetic component. Tissue, breed and environment effects need not transfer unchanged. Estimating annotation weights from the evaluation data itself also makes them fitted parameters, which belong inside the validation procedure. These issues connect directly to variance-component inference and genomic-feature priors.

3 Phenomics: breeding for trajectories and responses

Sensors, milking and feeding robots, cameras, spectra and laboratory assays can turn intermittently measured outcomes into repeated descriptions of behaviour, growth, physiological state and exposure. Automated feeding and robotic-milking studies already illustrate the derivation and genetic analysis of new traits (Bérat et al., 2025; Graham et al., 2024). These examples establish feasibility for particular measurements and populations, not universal benefit from every data stream.

An important prospective change is the target itself: from a lifetime total or a single observation towards a trajectory, stability across environments, response to challenge or recovery. Such targets could support breeding for robustness, health and resource efficiency. They require an explicit definition of exposure and opportunity to respond. A low observed disease count can reflect resistance, low exposure or incomplete recording.

Measurement becomes part of the inferential model. Device, farm and software effects may be associated with family, management and selection. More records do not automatically separate these effects, and a million sensor observations do not represent a million independent animals. Joint genetic and environmental factor models, random regression and observation models for events or counts are therefore likely to matter alongside conventional repeatability models. The phenomics chapter develops these choices and their measurement, missingness and validation requirements.

The strategic implication is that well-designed phenotyping may become a more valuable marginal investment than increasing marker density on an already well-genotyped reference. That is a resource-allocation hypothesis to test for each breeding programme, rather than a general ordering of technologies.

4 ML and AI: several distinct contributions

AI has at least four roles that should be evaluated separately:

Role Potential contribution Evidence needed
Measurement and feature extraction Derive traits from images, sound, spectra and time series Reference-measurement agreement and stability across farms, devices and biological groups
Molecular representation Predict regulatory consequences or learn sequence features Species-, tissue- and variant-specific assessment, followed by trait-level validation
Genetic and phenotypic prediction Learn nonlinear effects, interactions and heterogeneous responses Comparison with strong statistical baselines under the intended information pattern
Scientific software assistance Help develop code, interfaces, documentation and analysis workflows Independent numerical references, reproducible builds, tests and review

Sequence-to-function systems illustrate the second role. AlphaGenome combines long sequence context with multiple molecular prediction tasks (Avsec et al., 2026). Its reported human and mouse work is evidence about regulatory prediction, not evidence that the same model predicts livestock breeding values or crop performance. Adapting such representations to agricultural species requires appropriate training information and validation.

A promising design is a hybrid: learned measurement or biological representations feed a statistical model that explicitly accounts for relationships, repeated records, selection and uncertainty. Other applications may justify end-to-end prediction. Neither architecture is intrinsically best. Compare the quantity that will be used in selection, not only a proxy loss function. Predicting an animal’s phenotype from contemporaneous sensor data is a different task from predicting its transmissible genetic merit before those measurements are available.

For estimation, flexible learned effects can also compete with genetic and environmental covariance components. Model capacity does not resolve identifiability. Uncertainty must account for relevant training and tuning, and validation must protect against repeated animals, relatives, farms, time periods and pretrained-data overlap where these threaten the intended comparison. See model checking and validation.

AI-assisted software development may shorten implementation cycles. It also makes independent qualification more important: plausible code and a successful fit do not establish the equations solved, the statistical target or the validity of a reported uncertainty interval. The qualification framework applies regardless of how the implementation was produced.

5 Lower-cost genomics: coverage, design and uncertainty

Historical sequencing-cost data document major technological cost reductions, but those data do not constitute current agricultural service prices or the complete cost of collecting and analysing a sample (National Human Genome Research Institute). Future budgets must include sampling, extraction, libraries, failed assays, reference panels, computation and storage. Falling cost per base does not guarantee falling cost per useful breeding decision.

Arrays, low-pass sequencing with imputation, and longer-read technologies offer different information. Lamb et al. (2021) provide a proof of concept for cattle genomic prediction using low-coverage portable Nanopore sequencing. Wider adoption must still be justified for the population, variant classes, reference panel and quality requirements involved. Structural variation and more representative genome references are further opportunities, with corresponding demands on variant representation and model comparability.

If genomic information becomes affordable for more commercial animals, the reference can better represent deployment conditions. This could improve evaluation of health and environmental traits outside elite nuclei. It also creates design choices: more individuals, greater sequencing depth, wider environmental coverage, more informative relatives, or better phenotypes. Evaluate these choices jointly. Imputation quality and rare-variant coverage can differ by ancestry, so a larger reference is not automatically an equitable or representative one.

Methods and software will need to preserve dosage and imputation uncertainty where consequential, support evolving variant sets, and distinguish population coverage from marker count. The scientific requirements remain those in genomic models and missing data and selection.

6 Structural change: who records, learns and benefits?

Farm size, specialisation, automation and contractual relationships affect which animals are recorded and how genetics are deployed. The direction and pace vary between species, regions and production systems. Where farms adopt integrated recording systems, connected data streams can improve evaluation; where recording is concentrated in a narrow subset of farms, the apparent information gain can coincide with a narrower representation of the target population.

Breeding organisations may combine genetic resources, proprietary reference data, phenotyping platforms and analytical services. As one documented company example, Genus describes investment in precision data collection, genomic evaluation and additional trait streams (Genus plc, 2025). This is company-reported strategy, not independent evidence of comparative predictive performance.

Consolidation can finance expensive research and connected reference populations, but it can also change access, bargaining power and incentives to share data. The OECD’s seed-market analysis documents concentration and policy responses, while finding that its relationship with prices and innovation is not uniform (OECD, 2018). That plant-sector evidence should not be treated as a measured effect for every livestock industry, or as a prediction that consolidation will necessarily improve or damage genetic progress.

Cooperatives, public infrastructure, commercial platforms and shared research consortia offer different ways to organise these resources. The statistical consequences include selective recording, changing connectedness, proprietary calibration procedures and restricted opportunities for external validation. Cross-organisation analysis may require federated or distributed methods, but moving computation instead of records does not automatically solve privacy, harmonisation or population-comparability problems.

Useful agreements specify access to raw and derived records, consent and permitted use, correction of errors, portability, provenance and independent assessment. Open interfaces and auditable exports can preserve scientific comparability even when the underlying data cannot be public.

7 From improved prediction to sustained genetic progress

For a simplified single-trait selection scheme, expected response per year is often expressed as

\[ \Delta G_{\mathrm{year}}\approx \frac{i\,r\,\sigma_A}{L}, \]

where \(i\) is selection intensity, \(r\) the accuracy of the selection criterion, \(\sigma_A\) additive genetic standard deviation and \(L\) generation interval. This is a schematic decomposition, not a complete account of overlapping generations, multiple pathways, mating or multitrait constraints; its selection-index foundations are discussed by Hazel (1943). Breeding objectives, selection and mating develops the response algebra, contribution constraints and mating decisions.

The drivers can act on different terms. Earlier genomic information can shorten generation intervals; broader recording can improve accuracy; wider candidate screening can change selection intensity. Gains cannot be added independently: shorter cycles and strong selection can also accelerate relatedness and reduce future variation. Multi-objective selection and mating design therefore need to account for inbreeding, diversity, welfare and robustness alongside current production merit.

Changing farming systems also changes the target environment. A genotype ranked for performance in a high-input nucleus may not have the same ranking under heat, disease or resource limitation. Modelling genotype-by-environment interaction and validating transfer become important when the future production system differs from the reference. See multitrait and longitudinal models.

8 Implications for methods and software

The resulting research agenda is broader than replacing BLUP with a single new algorithm. It includes biologically informed hierarchical models, structured multitrait and longitudinal covariance, uncertainty for learned or imputed inputs, transfer across populations and environments, and breeding-programme design. Prediction and variance-component estimation need separate qualification; estimating more biological components requires information to identify them.

Software should make these developments assessable through stable model and data contracts, explicit prediction targets, modular observation and covariance models, versioned feature pipelines and reproducible execution. Large-scale inference needs matrix-free or sparse representations, controlled approximation, hardware-aware execution and transparent resource accounting. New computing hardware is useful where measured end-to-end workloads benefit, rather than because a method has an AI label. The large-scale computation chapter develops these requirements.

For gsuite, these are directions for continued development and qualification, not a claim that every model described here is already implemented. The useful long-term contract is that a user can identify the fitted model, trace its data, understand its assumptions, assess uncertainty and compare its results with an independent reference.

9 Plausible outcomes and evidence to seek

Several outcomes can coexist: integrated commercial platforms, shared public functional resources, and locally adapted programmes using interoperable tools. Their relative success will depend on reliable phenotypes, population coverage, economics and governance as well as predictive algorithms. No single ownership structure or model family follows inevitably from technological progress.

Evidence of progress should therefore include prospective accuracy and calibration, realised response and correlated responses, diversity, transfer to commercial conditions, and the total cost and distribution of benefits. A programme that improves a retrospective prediction score without improving selection decisions has not yet demonstrated the relevant breeding benefit.
