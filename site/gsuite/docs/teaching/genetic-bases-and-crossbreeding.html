9. Genetic bases, groups, metafounders and crossbreeding

Advanced notes: contents · Abbreviations

1 A breeding value needs a reference population

Additive genetic effects, relationship coefficients and genetic variances are defined relative to a reference. Conventional pedigree relationships usually take recorded founders to be unrelated and non-inbred relative to their stated base. That is a modelling convention about unrecorded ancestry, not a claim that the animals have no common ancestors.

Genomic centering also selects a reference: changing allele frequencies changes the coordinate system and potentially the covariance model. A statement such as “heritability is 0.3” or “these animals are unrelated” is incomplete without the trait scale, base and population assumptions. See genomic models for marker coding and normalization.

Distinguish the genetic base from the reporting base. Subtracting a common reference-cohort mean from displayed breeding values can leave all pairwise differences unchanged. Changing founder relationships, population means or genetic variance is a change to the model unless an explicit transformation establishes equivalence.

For a pure coordinate transformation \(a^*=s a+c\mathbf1\), with fixed \(s,c\), the covariance becomes \(s^2\operatorname{Var}(a)\). Prediction errors and their covariances must transform consistently. A group-specific base change is not generally represented by one common display offset.

2 Genetic groups describe base mean differences

One genetic-group representation is

\[ a=Q_g g+v,\qquad E(v)=0, \]

where \(g\) contains base-group means and \(Q_g\) contains inherited group contributions. The deviations \(v\) require a declared covariance model. This separates a mean model from the within- and across-group covariance assumptions. Genetic groups in an animal model are developed by Westell et al. (1988).

Unknown parents may be assigned to groups using defensible information such as origin, cohort or selection pathway. Contributions are then propagated through known ancestry. An observed breed label on each animal is not automatically the same design as ancestry contributions from unknown-parent groups.

If group effects are fixed, columns of \(ZQ_g\) can be confounded with the intercept or management effects. Constraints select a parameterization; they cannot create connectedness between otherwise disconnected populations. Compare estimable contrasts or total genetic values under aligned constraints rather than raw group and animal coefficients separately.

Assigning random priors to group means is a different hierarchical model. It imposes shrinkage and a covariance on those means. Merely calling a group “genetic” does not specify that covariance or distinguish mean differences from different genetic variances.

3 Metafounders describe ancestral covariance

Metafounders extend the ancestry model by representing relationships within and between ancestral populations (Legarra et al., 2015). A relationship matrix \(\Gamma\) describes those ancestral relationships. This differs from fitting fixed group means while retaining unrelated-founder covariance assumptions.

A schematic Gaussian decomposition illustrates the distinction:

\[ a=B a_M+\eta,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(a_M)=\sigma_A^2\Gamma,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(a_M,\eta)=0. \]

Then

\[ \operatorname{Var}(a)= \sigma_A^2B\Gamma B^\top+\operatorname{Var}(\eta). \]

Here \(B\) propagates ancestral contributions. In an actual metafounder pedigree construction, the innovation variances in \(\eta\) must also follow the compatible inheritance recursion. One cannot generally obtain that model by adding an arbitrary low-rank matrix to an ordinary pedigree relationship while leaving all other quantities unchanged.

\(\Gamma\) needs an admissible covariance structure and an explicit scale. Its elements are not a table of observed breed proportions. Estimation from markers depends on the genomic reference convention; pedigree labels alone do not reveal the magnitude of ancestral genomic relationships. Genetic variance, inbreeding and relationship coefficients must be interpreted on the resulting base. Values from different base conventions should not be compared as though they measured an identical quantity.

Metafounders can address a specified ancestral-population problem. They do not repair incorrect parentage, eliminate selection-history requirements or supply missing cross-population validation.

4 Pedigree and genomic compatibility in single-step

In single-step models, \(A_{gg}\) and the replacement genotyped covariance must refer to compatible domains, scales and bases. Aligning average diagonal and off-diagonal moments can be useful, but does not guarantee that every population-specific covariance or allele-frequency difference is reconciled.

Genetic groups affect the mean structure; metafounders affect ancestral covariance. Their contributions must be carried consistently through pedigree elimination, genomic adjustment and the treatment of non-genotyped animals. An implementation that silently retains the old founder precision while changing the genomic base does not establish a coherent joint model.

Qualification should distinguish an equivalent reparameterization from a new base assumption. For the former, predictions and target covariance should agree after transformation. For the latter, differences may be scientifically intended and need population-level validation. The Schur-complement identities in genomic models still apply to a correctly specified joint covariance; they do not determine that covariance on their own.

5 Purebred merit and crossbred performance are different targets

An animal selected within a nucleus may be intended to improve performance of crossbred commercial offspring. Genetic merit for the two settings need not be perfectly correlated. Differences in allele frequencies, dominance and genetic background can matter, alongside differences in production environment. Joint genomic evaluation of purebred and crossbred performance is developed for a two-breed system by Christensen et al. (2014).

As a simple model for animals in one parental line, define genetic effects for purebred and crossbred performance jointly:

\[ \operatorname{Var} \begin{pmatrix}a^{P}\\a^{C}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_P^2&r_{PC}\sigma_P\sigma_C\\ r_{PC}\sigma_P\sigma_C&\sigma_C^2 \end{bmatrix}\otimes K. \]

This is a multitrait representation on a declared parental-animal domain, not a complete terminal-cross model. It makes the target distinction explicit: when \(r_{PC}\) is below one, selection on purebred merit alone is not equivalent to selection on crossbred merit. Estimation requires information connecting the two performance settings. Differences in management and recording should not automatically be attributed to breed-specific genetic effects.

More detailed models can distinguish breed of origin of alleles and the parental populations through which effects are transmitted. These require appropriate ancestry, phasing and relationship information, and uncertainty from inferred origin may be consequential. A pooled genomic kernel with a breed fixed effect is not automatically equivalent to such a model.

6 Breed composition, heterosis and non-additive effects

Breed composition can enter the mean model, while heterosis and recombination effects describe other features of a crossbreeding design. They should not be absorbed into an additive genetic effect merely because all are associated with ancestry.

For illustration, if parental breed-origin probabilities are \(q_s\) and \(q_d\) and the transmitted origins are independent, expected between-breed heterozygosity is

\[ h=1-\sum_k q_{s,k}q_{d,k}. \]

This gives \(h=1\) for a first cross between two distinct pure breeds and \(h=1/2\) for the corresponding backcross under these assumptions. Offspring breed fractions alone are insufficient to distinguish all crossing histories. This quantity concerns breed origin, not observed heterozygosity at every locus.

A regression on such a crossbreeding covariate is a mean model. A dominance relationship kernel instead specifies covariance among individual non-additive effects; it is not a substitute for every systematic crossbreeding effect. For genomic additive/dominance covariance, see Vitezica et al. (2013) and genomic models.

6.1 More molecular information does not remove population differences

An annotation derived in one breed or tissue can be used as a sharing hypothesis, but it does not establish identical effect sizes, enrichment or measurement properties in another population. Model cross-population borrowing through declared covariance or hierarchical assumptions and assess it against the relevant purebred or crossbred target.

Broader sequencing and commercial phenotyping can improve population coverage, while also introducing different imputation quality, devices and management conditions. Keep genetic-base compatibility separate from those observation and transfer assumptions. The genotype-uncertainty example and functional-prior chapter identify distinct parts of this joint problem.

7 Information, validation and reporting

Record founder definitions, group assignments, ancestry fractions, relationship normalization, genetic-variance scale, constraints and the intended offspring population. Retain stable animal identities when adding ancestral entities. Changing those entities must not silently change the interpretation of output rows or uncertainty targets.

Small references should check group-incidence propagation, covariance symmetry and admissibility, inheritance recursions and prediction transformations. Reference calculations for metafounders must implement the same innovation variance convention, not only match ancestral matrix dimensions.

Deployment validation should include relevant breeds, crossbred generations, management systems and cohorts. Random splits dominated by one connected line cannot establish across-line comparability. Assess bias and dispersion as well as correlation, and uncertainty of between-population contrasts where such comparisons are scientifically identifiable. See missing data and selection and model checking and validation.
