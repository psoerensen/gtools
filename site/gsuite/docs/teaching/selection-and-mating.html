10. Breeding objectives, selection response and mating decisions

Advanced notes: contents · Abbreviations

1 Prediction supplies information for a decision

A genetic evaluation estimates or predicts quantities under a model. A breeding programme must then decide which animals contribute, how often, to which mates, and towards which objective. The best estimate of one trait is not necessarily the best selection criterion for the programme.

Define a breeding objective as a function of genetic outcomes, costs and constraints. A common linear approximation is aggregate merit

\[ H=c^\top a, \]

where \(a\) contains additive genetic values for objective traits and \(c\) contains declared economic or scientific weights. The same mathematical form can represent different objectives. Units and reference conditions are therefore essential: a weight per kilogram is not a weight per genetic standard deviation. Hazel’s selection-index framework connects an objective to correlated selection information (Hazel, 1943).

The objective traits need not be the recorded traits. An inexpensive sensor feature can provide information about health without receiving its own economic weight. Conversely, an important but poorly measured welfare or environmental trait does not cease to be part of the objective because its prediction is difficult.

2 Combining information without counting it twice

Let \(z\) be centered information available at selection, with covariance \(P_z\), and let \(C_{az}=\operatorname{Cov}(a,z)\). A linear index \(I=b^\top z\) minimizing \(E[(H-I)^2]\) satisfies

\[ P_z b=C_{az}^\top c. \]

The off-diagonal covariances account for redundant information. Adding a new record does not contribute independent information merely because it came from a different device or file. A derived genomic score and an estimated breeding value (EBV) trained on the same records can overlap extensively.

If a coherent joint evaluation already supplies \(\hat a=E(a\mid\mathcal D)\), then the posterior-mean predictor of a linear objective is \(\hat H=c^\top\hat a\). Its prediction-error variance is

\[ \operatorname{PEV}(H)=c^\top C_{\rm PE}c, \]

using the complete trait prediction-error covariance matrix. Weighting each trait EBV again by its reliability does not generally recover this predictor. Reliability is relevant to uncertainty and decision risk, but is not a generic additional shrinkage coefficient. See prediction and uncertainty.

Combining published evaluations from different sources instead requires a model for their bases, information overlap and error covariance. Treating them as independent measurements without justification can overstate precision.

3 Expected response and correlated change

Under joint normality and truncation selection on \(I\), with standardized selection intensity \(i\), the expected vector response among selected parents is

\[ \Delta a=i\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(a,I)}{\sigma_I} =i\frac{C_{az}b}{\sqrt{b^\top P_zb}}. \]

The corresponding change in the objective is \(\Delta H=c^\top\Delta a\). The single-objective expression \(i\,r_{HI}\sigma_H\) follows when \(r_{HI}\) is the accuracy of the criterion for \(H\). These formulas require the stated distribution and selection design; they do not replace a population-dynamic model for every breeding scheme.

Genetic correlations determine correlated responses. Giving one trait zero objective weight does not guarantee zero change in that trait. Similarly, maximizing a weighted objective does not ensure that every individual trait moves in its preferred direction. Report the expected response vector, not only the index score.

Dividing by a generation interval \(L\) gives a schematic response per year. Real programmes may have distinct selection pathways, ages, overlapping generations and dissemination delays. Faster generation turnover can improve annual response but also increase the rate at which relatedness accumulates. Long-term comparisons need both calendar-time response and genetic diversity.

4 Objectives, restrictions and uncertainty

Economic weights can be local derivatives of programme profit with respect to trait performance, holding the declared production conditions fixed. Prices, costs, biological limits and farming systems can change those derivatives. Sensitivity analysis should therefore examine plausible alternative objectives rather than imply that one set of weights is permanent.

Some outcomes are better represented by explicit restrictions, such as a lower bound on an expected health response, than by a small compensatory weight. Desired-gain or restricted-index approaches impose different decision criteria from unconstrained economic maximization. If restrictions are incompatible with the available genetic variation and information, an optimizer cannot make them scientifically feasible.

Uncertainty in EBVs, covariance parameters and objective weights is also distinct. A posterior mean is optimal for some quadratic-loss prediction problems, not for every risk-sensitive decision. Alternatives may evaluate downside outcomes, uncertain constraints or expected utility. Their assumptions and consequences should be explicit; numerical optimization cannot determine the ethical or economic acceptability of a trade-off.

5 Contributions and the control of relatedness

Selecting a ranked list leaves the number of descendants per parent unspecified. Optimum-contribution selection makes those contributions part of the decision. Meuwissen develops selection for maximum gain under a prescribed inbreeding constraint (Meuwissen, 1997).

For a simplified diploid random-mating generation, let \(w_i\geq0\) be each candidate’s fraction of parental gene contributions, with \(\mathbf1^\top w=1\), and let \(A\) be an additive relationship matrix on a declared base. A schematic optimization is

\[ \max_w\; w^\top\hat H \quad\text{subject to}\quad \tfrac12 w^\top A w\leq f_{\max},\qquad \mathbf1^\top w=1,\quad w\geq0. \]

The factor \(1/2\) converts additive relationship to coancestry in this convention. Sex-specific contribution totals, reproductive capacity, existing cohorts and other practical bounds usually add constraints. Expected group coancestry is a diversity criterion, not automatically the realised inbreeding of every planned offspring or an exact annual inbreeding rate. A rate constraint needs a reference cohort and a time or generation definition.

Using genomic rather than pedigree relationships changes which sharing is penalized, and its normalization and base matter. A numerical coancestry limit cannot be transferred blindly between different relationship scales. See genetic bases and crossbreeding. The variance available after several rounds of selection also need not remain equal to the initial reference variance.

6 Mating allocation is a further decision

Given selected parental contributions, a mating plan allocates pairs. Under a purely additive model, the expected offspring breeding value from parents \(s,d\) is \((\hat a_s+\hat a_d)/2\), but the Mendelian sampling variance and prediction uncertainty still affect offspring distributions. If an EBV omits a base-group contribution, that deviation alone is not the required total parental value.

For conventional pedigree relationships, expected offspring inbreeding is \(F_{sd}=A_{sd}/2\). This statement uses the corresponding pedigree base and inheritance assumptions; a genomic relationship coefficient on another scale should not be inserted without establishing its interpretation.

Allocation can reduce close matings while retaining selected contributions. It does not generally undo the longer-term concentration of ancestry created by heavy use of a few parents. Conversely, controlling average coancestry does not rule out every undesirable individual mating.

Recessive defects, dominance, heterosis and breed-specific commercial targets can introduce pair-specific objectives and restrictions. Their prediction requires additional information and models. An additive EBV alone does not identify the best pair for a non-additive outcome. Crossbreeding systems also need to distinguish parental purebred merit from expected crossbred performance.

7 Learning from realised outcomes

Selection changes the data subsequently observed. Records from selected parents or surviving offspring are not a random sample of the original population. Evaluation of realised genetic trends must distinguish genetic change from management change and account for the information used in selection. Forward validation and prospective outcome tracking complement retrospective accuracy comparisons; see missing data and selection and model checking and validation.

Assess gain, correlated responses, diversity, inbreeding, realised contributions and delivery to the commercial population. A favourable index trend in a nucleus does not establish that all intended outcomes reached farms, especially when environments or crossing systems differ.

8 What a reproducible decision analysis should retain

Keep the objective definition and weights, trait units and reference population, the joint evaluation and uncertainty source, candidate eligibility, relationship matrix convention, contribution bounds, mating restrictions and time horizon. Separate predicted outcomes from realised outcomes and model assumptions from operational constraints.

Small independent examples should verify index weights and response algebra, coancestry normalization, feasibility and the effect of changing constraints. Population simulations can then assess multi-generation gain, variation and uncertainty under a specified breeding scheme. These are qualifications of a decision procedure, beyond qualification of the underlying prediction solver.

NoteImplementation in gsuite

Genetic predictions and selected joint contrasts from standalone output and prediction uncertainty can supply inputs to an index or decision analysis. The reviewed interfaces do not establish a complete optimum-contribution selection or mating-allocation optimizer. Those decision procedures require additional constraints and qualification beyond an accepted mixed-model fit.
